# 08 — Temples and Frontal Scalp: A Separate Problem

The vertex and the frontal/temporal scalp behave differently enough that they need separate plans. Almost all the headline AGA trial data is **vertex** data, because that's the endpoint regulators approved on. Applying vertex expectations to temples leads to quitting a working drug.

---

## Why temples are harder

| Factor | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Highest androgen receptor density** on the scalp | First area to miniaturize, most aggressively |
| **First to go, so longest affected** | By the time recession is *noticed*, those follicles have been miniaturizing for years — less salvageable follicle mass remains than at a crown of equivalent visible severity |
| Vertex follicles miniaturize later | So a newly-noticed crown has more recoverable material than a newly-noticed temple |
| Cochrane-noted evidence gap | The minoxidil evidence base for crown use is substantially larger and more consistent than for frontal thinning |

**Practical translation:** the same drug, same adherence, same duration produces a *smaller* visible gain at the temples than at the crown. That is expected, not failure. Judge the two regions against different yardsticks.

---

## First: is it even AGA? (do this before treating)

Three things look like androgenetic temple recession and respond to completely different treatment. **A dermoscopy exam plus family history is how a dermatologist separates them** — worth one appointment before spending 18 months on the wrong target.

### 1. Mature hairline (normal, permanent, untreatable)

- The natural rounding of the hairline corners that most men develop in their **late teens and twenties**
- Moves back roughly **1–2 cm from the juvenile position, then stops**
- **This is not AGA and no drug will bring it back.** Chasing it with minoxidil is a permanent, guaranteed disappointment

**The discriminator:** true AGA recession is **progressive** — it doesn't stabilize, it keeps advancing, and it shows **miniaturization (reduced shaft diameter) at the leading edge**. Photos 6 months apart show measurable change. A mature hairline photographed 6 months apart looks identical.

This matters a lot for expectation-setting: **some fraction of most adult men's temple recession is maturation, and that fraction is permanently out of reach of drugs.** Only the progressive, miniaturizing component is treatable. Transplant is the only route for the rest.

### 2. Traction alopecia

Recession at exactly the temples/frontal margin from mechanical pull — tight caps, hats worn identically every day, pulled-back styles, aggressive combing in one direction, hard towel-drying. Frequently coexists with AGA and gets misattributed to it. Reversible early, permanent late. Costs nothing to fix.

### 3. Frontal fibrosing alopecia (rare in men, but changes everything)

A scarring, lymphocytic alopecia causing band-like frontal/temporal recession, often with **loss of sideburns and eyebrows** and **perifollicular erythema/scaling** at the margin. Rare in men but not absent.

**It requires anti-inflammatory/immunomodulatory treatment, not minoxidil, and it scars — meaning delay costs permanently.** If there's redness, scaling, itching or burning at the hairline, or sideburn/eyebrow loss, this needs a dermatologist promptly rather than another six months of self-treatment.

## What actually has frontal/temporal evidence

### Dutasteride — the strongest frontal data ⭐

This is the finding that most changes the temple calculus. From the 2019 systematic review/meta-analysis of dutasteride vs finasteride, reported **by region**:

| Endpoint | Frontal scalp | Vertex |
|---|---|---|
| Hair count, mean difference (duta vs fin) | **0.63** (95% CI 0.13–1.13, p = 0.01) | 0.68 (95% CI 0.13–1.23, p = 0.02) |
| Panel global photographic assessment, MD | **0.25** (95% CI 0.18–0.31, p < 0.00001) | 0.17 (95% CI 0.09–0.24, p < 0.00001) |

Two readings:

1. **Dutasteride beats finasteride at the frontal scalp**, significantly, on both hair count and blinded photographic assessment
2. On **global photographic assessment the frontal advantage is larger than the vertex advantage** (0.25 vs 0.17) — i.e. dutasteride's edge over finasteride may be *most* visible at the front

An open-label evaluator-blinded RCT (*IJDVL*) similarly reported dutasteride superiority for hair regrowth and reversal of miniaturization.

**⚠️ Direct consequence for anyone considering a dutasteride → finasteride step-down for side-effect reasons: if temples are a priority, that switch costs more than a crown-only analysis suggests.** This is a genuine trade-off, not a technicality. See [07-5ari-neuro-overlap.md](07-5ari-neuro-overlap.md) for how to weigh it — and the local-delivery option below, which may let you have both.

### Local dutasteride delivery — emerging, and directly relevant to this trade-off

If systemic dutasteride is the problem but frontal dutasteride is the benefit, delivering it locally is the obvious move. The evidence is early but real and growing:

- **Topical dutasteride solution (0.01%, 0.02%, 0.05% w/v)** — randomized, double-blind, placebo- **and** active-controlled **Phase II** study in men with AGA
- **Dutasteride mesotherapy** (intradermal microinjection) — **case series**, JAAD Case Reports 2024
- **Intralesional dutasteride** — **systematic review and meta-analysis** of effectiveness and safety

**Status: promising, not established.** Phase II plus a meta-analysis of small intralesional studies is a weaker footing than oral dutasteride's, and compounded topical formulations carry the same quality-control concern the FDA raised in 2025 about compounded topical finasteride. But as a way to concentrate anti-androgen at the temples while reducing systemic (and therefore CNS type-1) exposure, this is the most mechanistically appropriate option available, and worth raising with a dermatologist explicitly.

### Minoxidil — works frontally, just less

Frequently cited: 5% minoxidil solution improved frontal-scalp hair density in **58% of men at 48 weeks** (attributed to *JAAD*). ⚠️ *This figure circulates via clinic/commercial secondary sources; the primary paper was not verified for this document — treat the exact number as indicative, not established.*

The defensible statement: **minoxidil does produce frontal benefit; it is smaller and less consistently documented than vertex benefit.** Worth doing. Not worth expecting crown-magnitude results from.

**48 weeks is the timescale.** Frontal response is slower to become visible than vertex response, which means the temples are exactly where people quit too early.

### Microneedling — mechanically ideal for temples

The 2025 meta-analysis (12 RCTs, SMD 1.32 vs minoxidil alone) is whole-scalp AGA data, not temple-specific. But the temples are flat, thin-skinned, easy to reach precisely, and easy to treat symmetrically — the practical case is strong even though the regional data isn't separated. Depth and technique didn't significantly change outcomes in that analysis, so precision isn't the issue; consistency is.

⚠️ **Go gentler at the temples.** Thinner skin over bone, and the frontal/temporal region is more prone to visible irritation. Stay at the lower end (**1.0 mm**), and do not needle over any area with redness, scaling or active irritation.

### PRP — reasonable to bring forward for temples specifically

PRP is injected regionally, so it can be targeted at the temples directly. Given that drugs systematically **underperform** at the frontal scalp, the argument for adding PRP earlier here than at the crown is stronger than the general sequencing in [05-procedures.md](05-procedures.md) implies. Same caveats: modest, not durable, needs maintenance, **ask whether the PRP is activated**.

### Transplant — the temples are where surgery is actually good value

This inverts the crown advice. Compared with the crown, the temples/hairline are:

| | **Temples / hairline** | **Crown / vertex** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | A **line** — bounded, finite | A **hole that expands radially** with age |
| Graft count | Low: roughly **500–1,500** for temple corners | High: 2,000–2,500+, often needing a second pass |
| Predictability | High | Lower — whorl angles are technically demanding |
| Cosmetic impact per graft | **Highest on the scalp** — frames the face | Moderate |
| Donor efficiency | Excellent | Poor |
| Risk of chasing progression | Lower (bounded area) | High (keeps growing) |

**So the temples are the single most graft-efficient, predictable, high-impact transplant target — and they're also the area drugs help least.** Those two facts point the same direction: temples are where surgery earns its cost.

The stabilisation rule still holds absolutely: **12+ months of demonstrated stability on medical therapy first.** And the artistry requirement is higher — temple points and the frontal margin need fine single-follicle grafts placed at correct acute angles. A crown surgeon and a hairline artist are not interchangeable; ask specifically for **12-month post-op hairline/temple cases**.

⚠️ **Do not transplant a mature hairline back to its juvenile position.** It looks wrong at 30 and worse at 50, and it spends donor grafts that the crown will need. A surgeon who agrees to a low, straight, teenage hairline is telling you something about their judgment.

## On unilateral (one-sided) application and asymmetry

### Asymmetry is a documented, normal feature of AGA

- An observational study of 100 men (Norwood 2–5, **no prior medication or surgery**) found **77% had greater loss on the right side** and 23% greater on the left — assessing frontal symmetry only
- A separate quantitative study of frontal hairline regression in 41 patients described **asymmetrical hairline regression as a disorder-associated phenomenon with unknown biological causality**

**The key fact: that first study measured men who had never been treated.** Asymmetry is baseline AGA behaviour, present before any drug is applied. It needs no drug to explain it.

### The reverse-causation trap

The inference "the side I treated has less hair, therefore the treatment cost me hair" is the most common self-diagnosis error in topical therapy, and it usually has the causal arrow backwards:

```
What it feels like:   applied minoxidil → that side is now thinner
What usually happened: that side was already thinner → so that's where I applied minoxidil
                       → the treated side is still the thinner side
                       → "the minoxidil did this"
```

Application follows perceived loss. Then the treated area is compared against an area that was always better. Without a **baseline photo from before treatment**, the two explanations are indistinguishable — and the prior strongly favours pre-existing asymmetry, because that's documented in untreated men at high frequency.

### The one way patchy application genuinely can hurt

There is a real mechanism worth naming, distinct from the above:

**Repeated start–stop cycling.** Each minoxidil initiation can trigger a synchronised telogen release ("dread shed") at weeks 2–8, and each discontinuation loses the accumulated gains over the following months. Applying inconsistently — on for a few weeks, off for a few, unevenly across the scalp — can mean repeatedly paying the shed without ever banking the 6–12 month gain. Sustained inconsistency is plausibly worse than either steady use or no use.

⚠️ This is **mechanistic reasoning from established minoxidil pharmacology, not a trial finding.** No study has tested intermittent unilateral application. Treat it as a reason to be consistent, not as a diagnosis.

**What is well established: minoxidil does not cause permanent hair loss.** The shed is telogen synchronisation, and it reverses. Stopping minoxidil out of fear that it caused loss reliably produces the loss that was feared.

### The fix that removes the variable entirely

**Oral minoxidil is systemic — every follicle gets the same dose.** Application asymmetry, missed sides, uneven coverage and patchy adherence all cease to exist as variables. For anyone whose failure mode is inconsistent or uneven topical application, this is the single cleanest solution, and it resolves the temple-coverage problem at the same time (no more deciding whether to spend the dropper on the hairline or the crown).

If staying topical: **treat the entire androgen-sensitive scalp, both sides, every time.** Temples, frontal margin, mid-scalp, crown. Not just the visibly worst spot. Half the point of treatment is protecting follicles that haven't miniaturized yet — and there is no such thing as a scalp region that is thinning on only one side for biological reasons you can act on.

## The parallel temple protocol

Everything here runs **alongside** the main plan, not instead of it. Nothing in this list conflicts with the systemic stack.

| Track | Action | Evidence tier | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Confirm the diagnosis** | Dermoscopy: AGA vs mature hairline vs traction vs FFA. Establishes what's treatable before you spend 18 months on it | Standard of care | one consult |
| **2. Systemic anti-androgen** | The main frontal lever. **Dutasteride has the best frontal data.** If stepping down for side-effect reasons, weigh this explicitly | SR/MA | ~₹200–400/mo |
| **3. Minoxidil — go oral** | Removes application asymmetry and coverage trade-offs entirely; fixes adherence | Cohorts + consensus | ~₹300/mo |
| **4. Microneedling at temples** | 1.0 mm, weekly-to-fortnightly, gentler than crown, both sides equally, no topicals 24 h after | SR/MA (whole-scalp) | one-time ~₹800 |
| **5. Stop traction** | Vary cap use, no tight/pulled-back styles, no hard towel-drying or aggressive combing at the margin | Standard of care | free |
| **6. Ketoconazole 2%** | 2–3×/week, 3–5 min contact, include the hairline | SR | ~₹300 |
| **7. Local dutasteride** | Topical/mesotherapy/intralesional — ask a dermatologist. The route to frontal dutasteride benefit with less systemic exposure | **Phase II + case series + SR/MA of small studies — emerging** | varies |
| **8. Tretinoin 0.025%** | Only if on **topical** minoxidil and it's underperforming. Pointless with oral minoxidil. Extra caution on thin temple skin | One clinical study + review | ~₹200 |
| **9. PRP at temples** | Reasonable to bring **forward** vs the crown, because drugs underperform frontally. Ask if activated | SR/MA (43 RCTs) | ₹3,000–8,000/session |
| **10. Transplant, temples** | Best value on the scalp: ~500–1,500 grafts, bounded area, highest cosmetic return. **Only after 12+ months proven stability.** Demand 12-month temple cases | Established | ₹25,000–90,000 |

### Expectation setting

- **48 weeks**, not 6 months, before judging frontal response
- Expect a **smaller** gain than the crown at equal effort — that's the biology, not a failure
- The maturation component of the hairline **will not come back**. Only the progressive miniaturizing component is drug-addressable
- Temples are where people quit too early because they benchmarked against crown expectations. Don't
